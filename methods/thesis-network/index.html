The cognitive map in my thesis did not begin as a readable diagram. It developed through source selection, causal coding, concept integration, and repeated decisions about what to display. This article follows that process, showing the earlier figures alongside the maps used in the analysis.

Two kinds of change need to be distinguished. Integrating concepts and setting disconnected clusters aside changed the analytical representation. Moving nodes, changing their visual prominence, and selecting a smaller view changed how that representation could be read. Neither operation, by itself, established that Xi’s beliefs caused a policy decision.

01 / Selecting the corpus

Start with statements, not policy outcomes

I selected material that could reveal Xi’s expressed perceptions before the policy I was trying to explain. Chapter 2 reports that 190 sources were selected for closer examination. Thirteen Chinese-language interviews from 1989–2002 met the criteria for the core corpus: they distinguished the interviewer’s questions from Xi’s answers and allowed his own statements to be identified.

I also coded the two 2013 speeches introducing the land and maritime components of the Belt and Road Initiative, using their official English translations. These speeches connected the later initiative to the earlier interview map; they were not additional pre-policy interviews. The distinction matters because policy outcomes should not be used to manufacture the beliefs that supposedly explain them.

Chapter 2 · Text Selection and Sampling Strategy; Chapter 4 · Data Presentation

02 / The unit of analysis

Code a relationship, not just a theme

My basic unit was a causal relationship: a cause concept, an effect concept, and the sign of the connection between them. I followed the cognitive-mapping coding procedures discussed in Chapter 2, including Wrightson’s rules. A passage was relevant when its wording expressed a causal or explanatory relationship; simply mentioning development or cooperation was not enough.

I recorded whether the cause was understood to increase, decrease, or have a neutral relationship with the effect. The coding scheme used positive, negative, and neutral links. A negative sign indicates an inhibiting relationship; it does not mean that a concept or policy is morally undesirable. The coding sheet retains interview and paragraph references so the recorded relationship can be checked against its source.

03 / From 612 to 326 concepts

Integrate similar expressions without erasing distinctions

I initially kept phrases close to the source wording. That preserved distinctions during coding, but it also allowed slightly different expressions of a similar idea to appear as separate nodes. I then reviewed these expressions and selected representative labels where their meanings and functions were sufficiently close.

Chapter 4 gives the example of expanding industrial access, industrial upgrading, and industrial development being consolidated under industrial development. It reports that concept integration reduced 612 initial nodes to 326. This panel illustrates that reported decision; it does not reconstruct a missing 612-node layout or claim to provide a complete record of every merge. 04 / Importing the coded network

The first graph was not yet an argument

I organized the coded relationships in Excel and imported the nodes and directed links into Gephi. Repeated relationships were aggregated into weighted links, and incoming and outgoing links supplied degree measures. The retained workbook contains 326 concepts and 453 directed links.

Figure 7-2 is the actual unadjusted graph reproduced in Appendix B. Larger nodes and heavier lines draw attention to degree and repetition, but the dense layout makes chains of reasoning difficult to follow. A visually prominent concept is not yet an explanation of how the surrounding argument works. Link weight records repetition in the coded texts; it is not an estimated causal effect.

05 / Separating the clusters

Use layout to see the structure

Figure 7-3 shows an intermediate layout using Yifan Hu. Spreading the graph makes a distinction visible that the original image obscured: a large main component and several small groups disconnected from it. The algorithm helped expose the structure; it did not decide which concepts mattered to the research question.

Appendix B treats controlled repositioning as an analytical task informed by the coding. Placing causes and effects so their paths can be followed makes the map more intelligible, while leaving the direction and sign of each coded relationship intact. The final arrangement was therefore not simply the output of an automatic layout algorithm.

06 / The main component

Set disconnected groups aside for the analysis

The small disconnected groups did not contribute to interpreting the main structure of Xi’s cognitive map. Appendix B lists the relationships set aside and explains that they were removed from the focused analytical map. This was a decision about relevance to this research question, rather than a claim that those statements were invalid.

The published main component contains 282 concepts. The complete workbook retains another 44 concepts outside it. This view shows the main component in its published arrangement. The full-workbook control and concept index keep the other records available, so the analytical reduction remains distinguishable from the underlying coded material.

07 / Structural analysis with R and igraph

Locate reciprocal paths, then assess their meaning

I used R and igraph to examine relationships that were difficult to identify by reading the full map. Strongly connected components identify concepts that can reach one another through directed paths. They help locate reciprocal causal reasoning; they do not establish the truth of the beliefs or make every concept in the displayed cluster equally important.

Appendix B presents Figure 7-4 as the central analytical cluster. I reproduce its 42 displayed concepts here. In the current workbook, 38 belong to the strongly connected component containing development; lawful administration, government transparency, complementarity, and diversity supply additional context in the published figure. The display therefore should not be confused with an exact list of the algorithm’s component membership.

08 / From network structure to interpretation

Build a smaller map around a question

For each question, I followed incoming and outgoing paths and selected the concepts needed to understand the relationship. When several concepts were involved, I used network analysis to identify their connections, then checked the resulting cluster against the qualitative reading of the speeches and interviews. Nodes without a significant semantic connection to that question were set aside in the smaller map.

This is the map used to examine public opinion and governance. Its arrangement makes the relationship among people, administrative practices, obstacles, and development easier to follow. In reduced maps where degree and repetition were not the analytical issue, I equalized node sizes and line thicknesses. That directs attention to the paths rather than encouraging readers to treat size as the argument.

09 / Signs and chains

Read indirect effects without inventing new links

A chain of negative links needs to be read step by step. Appendix B discusses how excessive planned economy weakens the administrative management system, that system reduces corruption, and corruption weakens public opinion. Weakening a restraint on corruption is different from directly reducing corruption.

Appendix C explains direct and indirect relationships through signed matrices and an illustrative feedback loop. That example explains the method; it is not additional Xi data. Here, indirect reasoning remains a path through recorded relationships, rather than being drawn as an invented direct edge or calculated as a real-world effect size.

10 / Checking the interpretation

Return the argument to the full map

Appendix B proposes placing the selected clusters back into the full cognitive map to look for overlooked relationships. This return matters: a small map makes an argument readable, but selection can also hide connections that complicate it. The full record allows those choices to be inspected.

The result is a structured interpretation of Xi’s expressed causal beliefs, not direct access to his private thoughts and not proof that those beliefs caused the Belt and Road Initiative. Chapter 2 treats the map as a source of testable expectations and addresses causal inference separately through process tracing. The research article shows how I used the resulting maps to examine development, legitimacy, cooperation, and order.

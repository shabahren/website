Research

Chinese foreign policy through perceptions, narratives, and international interaction.

My research investigates how interpretations of international politics shape Chinese foreign policy and China’s relations with the Middle East. I study both the beliefs through which policymakers define foreign-policy problems and the narratives through which states communicate identity, order, and legitimate action.

A connected research program

Foreign policy is not produced by material conditions alone. Leaders and institutions interpret those conditions, identify problems, define possible responses, and communicate their choices through broader narratives. These narratives can then be accepted, contested, adapted, or used strategically by other actors.

My research follows this process from the formation of Chinese foreign-policy ideas to their international communication and reception. The Middle East provides an important setting in which to examine how Chinese concepts interact with different political identities, development strategies, security concerns, and relationships with the existing international order.

Current research questions

  1. How do leadership perceptions and causal beliefs shape the interpretation of China’s foreign-policy environment?
  2. How does China narrate its identity, development experience, and role in international order?
  3. How do Middle Eastern actors interpret, selectively adopt, resist, or strategically appropriate Chinese narratives?
  4. When does rhetorical alignment indicate a deeper change in causal beliefs, and when does it primarily reflect material or diplomatic calculation?
  5. Under what conditions can changes in interpretation influence subsequent foreign-policy choices?

Research themes

Chinese Foreign Policy and Decision-Making

I examine how Chinese leaders interpret international change and connect external conditions to domestic priorities, political legitimacy, and available policy instruments. My work on Xi Jinping and the Belt and Road Initiative uses cognitive mapping to reconstruct causal beliefs and show how leadership interpretation can complement structural and institutional explanations of policy formation.

Key concepts: foreign-policy analysis · leadership perceptions · causal beliefs · cognitive mapping · policy formation

China–Middle East Relations

I study China’s expanding relations with Middle Eastern actors, particularly Iran and Saudi Arabia. My work considers economic diplomacy, development strategies, regional order, and the interaction between Chinese narratives and local political priorities. I focus on how regional actors interpret and reshape the meaning of engagement with China.

Key concepts: China–Iran relations · China–Saudi relations · Belt and Road Initiative · economic diplomacy · regional order

Strategic Narratives and International Order

I examine how Chinese political actors diagnose problems in the existing international order, define China’s identity and responsibilities, and describe legitimate directions of international change. I am particularly interested in narratives of national rejuvenation, the Belt and Road Initiative, a community with a shared future for mankind, development, sovereignty, and multipolarity.

Key concepts: strategic narratives · international order · identity · legitimacy · narrative reception

Images, Language, and Political Interpretation

My earlier research on China’s international image, Middle East studies in China, and political translation informs a broader interest in how language shapes political possibility. This includes competition over national images, politically consequential wording, and the role of linguistic and cultural knowledge in foreign-policy analysis.

Key concepts: national image · political language · translation · representation · cross-cultural interpretation

Completed foundation: M.A. research

My M.A. thesis, Xi Jinping’s Perceptions and the Belt and Road Initiative, examined how leadership interpretation contributed to the formation of one of China’s most consequential foreign-policy initiatives. Using qualitative analysis and cognitive mapping, I reconstructed causal relations expressed in selected speeches and interviews.

The study shows how Xi’s causal beliefs connected international order, development, national rejuvenation, and political legitimacy. It argues that these beliefs helped organize inherited and previously fragmented policy instruments within a broader political framework. The thesis does not treat leadership cognition as a complete explanation; instead, it demonstrates how cognitive analysis can clarify the interpretive mechanisms connecting structural conditions to policy priorities and choices.

From Development Partner to Order-Shaping Actor: China’s Strategic Narrative in the Middle East

China’s Middle East narrative increasingly links development and sovereignty to selective diplomacy and a multipolar regional order.

Research problem

China’s official discourse toward the Middle East has expanded considerably under Xi Jinping. Earlier interpretations generally treated Beijing’s regional policy as an energy-focused strategy based on trade, political neutrality, and avoidance of military commitments. These elements remain important, but they no longer capture the full narrative architecture through which China presents its regional role.

Since the launch of the Belt and Road Initiative, Chinese statements have increasingly connected economic cooperation to sovereignty, civilizational pluralism, regional security, Palestinian statehood, diplomatic reconciliation, and the construction of a more multipolar international order. The 2016 China’s Arab Policy Paper placed the Belt and Road Initiative at the centre of China–Arab cooperation while reaffirming non-interference and the Five Principles of Peaceful Coexistence.1 Subsequent speeches and initiatives gradually added a more explicit political and security dimension.

The resulting research problem is not simply whether China has become more influential. It is how Beijing narratively reconciles two positions that may be in tension: its continued rejection of intervention and alliance politics, and its growing claim to facilitate regional security, diplomatic settlement, and international-order reform.

METHOD


This note proposes analysing Chinese policy papers, summit speeches, diplomatic agreements, and mediation initiatives as components of a layered strategic narrative. Official claims should be compared with regional reception and evidence of material implementation. The objective is not to assume that Chinese discourse directly causes political outcomes, but to identify how it defines China’s identity, diagnoses regional problems, and legitimizes particular forms of involvement.

Developing argument

China’s strategic narrative in the Middle East may be understood as an evolving combination of four connected claims.

First, China presents development as the principal foundation of regional stability. The 2016 policy paper formalized a “1+2+3” cooperation structure in which energy constituted the core, infrastructure and trade formed two supporting wings, and advanced technologies represented emerging areas of cooperation.1 This framework implies that insecurity is partly produced by insufficient development rather than primarily by regime type. It resembles what Degang Sun describes as “peace through development” or developmental peace.2

Second, China presents sovereignty and non-interference as the political conditions of legitimate cooperation. This differentiates its narrative from Western approaches associated in Chinese discourse with intervention, political conditionality, and externally imposed institutional change. Wang Yi’s 2021 five-point initiative argued that Middle Eastern states should independently select their political systems and development paths while pursuing security through dialogue, non-proliferation, justice for Palestine, and economic development.3

Third, China increasingly connects regional partnerships to multipolarity and international-order reform. Under this framing, China–Arab cooperation is not only bilateral or economic. It becomes evidence that developing countries can participate in constructing an international order based on sovereign equality, civilizational diversity, and resistance to hegemonism. Xi Jinping’s 2022 China–Arab States Summit speech incorporated this language into the idea of a “China-Arab community with a shared future.”4 At the 2024 China–Arab States Cooperation Forum, the discourse was expanded through proposed frameworks covering innovation, finance, energy, trade, and people-to-people relations.5

Fourth, Beijing uses selective diplomatic activism as evidence of responsible power. The 2023 Saudi–Iran agreement gave China an unusually visible role in regional diplomacy.6 Its significance for China’s narrative may exceed its direct causal role in producing the agreement. The event enabled Beijing to present dialogue, sovereignty, and non-interference as practical alternatives to coercion and alliance-based competition.

The narrative nevertheless remains modular rather than uniform. China emphasizes modernization, energy, and national development strategies in its relations with Gulf monarchies. Relations with Iran are more easily connected to sovereignty, sanctions resistance, and opposition to hegemonic pressure. Israel has historically been framed through innovation and technological cooperation, while Türkiye is approached through logistics and connectivity. Palestine, by contrast, functions as a normative anchor for China’s broader claims about justice and regional order.

This variation suggests that Chinese narratives are not merely transmitted to passive audiences. Regional governments select the components that support their own domestic legitimacy, economic programmes, and diplomatic positioning. Research on Chinese narrative projection in the Gulf demonstrates that local actors reproduce some Chinese claims while adapting or contesting others.7 Narrative reception should therefore be studied as negotiation rather than acceptance.

Connection to my research

This note connects strategic-narrative research with the study of China’s foreign policy and China–Middle East relations. It provides a possible framework for examining how Chinese narratives are received and repurposed by regional actors rather than measuring only whether they are successfully disseminated.

A comparative project could distinguish among three levels:

  1. China’s international-system narrative about multipolarity and global governance.

  2. China’s regional narrative about development, sovereignty, and collective security.

  3. Country-specific narratives adapted to Saudi Arabia, Iran, Egypt, the UAE, Israel, Türkiye, Syria, or Lebanon.

This framework could also support research on self-reinforcing or self-sealing narratives. Diplomatic successes may be interpreted as confirmation that China’s non-interference model works, while failures may be attributed to external intervention, Western hegemony, or inadequate development. The important question is whether this narrative structure allows contradictory outcomes to reinforce the same underlying interpretation.

Limitations

LIMITATION


Official discourse cannot establish China’s intentions, regional influence, or responsibility for diplomatic outcomes. Agreements such as the Saudi–Iran normalization process involved earlier negotiations and regional incentives that cannot be reduced to Chinese mediation. Narrative prominence also does not demonstrate material capacity.

The available literature is unevenly distributed. Gulf states receive substantially more attention than Iran, Türkiye, Syria, Lebanon, or non-state audiences. English-language research may also overlook disagreements within Chinese academic and policy communities.

The concept of developmental peace requires further specification. It is not yet clear whether it constitutes a coherent causal theory, a diplomatic slogan, or a flexible legitimizing framework. Similarly, Chinese mediation may remain a form of limited conflict management rather than a willingness to guarantee or enforce settlements. Chaziza’s concept of “quasi-mediation” is useful because it describes engagement that raises China’s diplomatic visibility while limiting its political and reputational exposure.8

The central hypothesis should therefore remain provisional: China appears to be moving from a narrative of economic reassurance toward one of selective order-shaping, but the relationship between narrative ambition and sustained material commitment has not been established.

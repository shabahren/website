Research Note · Research Note 3

Beyond Rhetorical Adoption: Chinese Narratives and Elite Causal Beliefs in Iran and Saudi Arabia

Chinese ideational influence should be measured through changes in elite causal beliefs, not rhetorical agreement or policies favourable to Beijing.

Research problem

Studies of China’s international influence frequently move too quickly from observable accommodation to claims about ideational influence. Governments may support the Belt and Road Initiative, repeat Chinese diplomatic language, or adopt policies favourable to Beijing because cooperation provides trade, investment, infrastructure finance, diplomatic protection, or strategic alternatives. Such behaviour may reflect structural dependence or calculated alignment rather than acceptance of China’s interpretation of international politics.

The distinction has become increasingly important because China no longer presents itself only as an economic partner. Through the Belt and Road Initiative and the Global Development, Security, and Civilization Initiatives, Beijing promotes a broader account of how development, sovereignty, security, civilizational diversity, and international order are causally connected. The language of a “community with a shared future for humanity” is therefore not merely promotional. It advances an interpretation of what produces instability, what legitimate governance requires, and how international order should be organized.

The central argument of this note is that Chinese ideational influence should not be measured primarily through rhetorical repetition, participation in Chinese initiatives, policy alignment, or support for Chinese leadership. It should instead be assessed through changes in the causal beliefs by which foreign elites connect political concepts and explain policy problems.

METHOD


Ideational influence can be investigated by comparing the causal maps contained in Chinese official narratives with those expressed by foreign decision-makers over time. Cognitive mapping can identify whether elites begin to connect development, security, sovereignty, modernization, and international order in ways resembling Chinese discourse. Process tracing is then required to determine whether such changes followed exposure to Chinese narratives and entered actual policy reasoning.

Developing argument

The existing literature on Chinese strategic narratives can be divided into three overlapping strands.

The first examines narrative content and transmission. Chinese party-to-party relations, elite networks, political training, exchange programmes, and diplomatic forums provide channels through which Chinese political concepts are communicated abroad.1 These narratives seek to strengthen Beijing’s agenda-setting and rule-making capacity, contest the “China threat” narrative, reinterpret disputed political values, and associate China’s development achievements with governance under the Chinese Communist Party.2

The second strand examines reception. Research across Belt and Road partner countries shows that Chinese narratives are rarely accepted or rejected in their entirety. They are selectively interpreted, translated, and instrumentalized. Political elites may welcome Chinese economic engagement while remaining unwilling to accept a regional order dominated by China.3 Governments may adapt Chinese narratives to support their own domestic objectives, strategic identities, or claims to political legitimacy.4 They may also adopt individual concepts while rejecting the political meaning Beijing attaches to them.5

The third strand explains this selective reception through material interests, geopolitical calculation, identity compatibility, and ontological security. Narratives are likely to resonate when they are useful and compatible with existing self-understandings. They encounter resistance when they threaten autonomy, identity, political status, or established interpretations of national interests.6

These approaches explain why Chinese narratives receive uneven responses, but they frequently evaluate influence through unsuitable indicators. Rhetorical convergence does not necessarily indicate that elites have adopted Chinese causal reasoning. Governments may repeat the language of “win-win cooperation,” multipolarity, or sovereignty because it is diplomatically useful. Similarly, policies favourable to China may result from material incentives or strategic necessity.

The reverse problem is equally important. Foreign elites may adopt causal assumptions resembling China’s while continuing to oppose a China-led order. They may accept that:

Thinking in terms similar to China does not necessarily produce conclusions favourable to China. Conversely, conclusions favourable to China do not necessarily demonstrate thinking in Chinese terms.

This distinction creates a useful comparison between Iran and Saudi Arabia.

Iran initially appears to be a most-likely case for Chinese ideational influence. It faces Western sanctions, maintains a comprehensive strategic partnership with China, and shares elements of Beijing’s discourse concerning sovereignty, anti-unilateralism, non-interference, and opposition to hegemonic international structures. Recent research identifies substantial discursive convergence between Iranian and Chinese narratives.7 Yet rhetorical similarity does not establish that Iranian elites have reorganized their causal understanding of foreign policy. Iranian reasoning, including in nuclear diplomacy and regional security, may remain primarily centred on deterrence, threat, resistance, and regime security.

Saudi Arabia presents a less obvious but potentially more significant case. It remains closely connected to the United States, yet Vision 2030-era discourse increasingly links economic development, technological modernization, political stability, diversified partnerships, and international status. This configuration appears compatible with Chinese narratives that connect infrastructure-led development to security and national rejuvenation. Research on China–Saudi relations suggests that material projects are embedded in a shared temporal and spatial narrative about modernization and future-oriented state transformation.8

The comparison therefore produces a counter-intuitive expectation:

A geopolitically aligned partner may reproduce Chinese language without substantially adopting Chinese causal reasoning, while a traditionally US-aligned partner may incorporate Chinese-like causal assumptions into its account of development and international order.

The important issue is not which country is more pro-China. It is whether elite understandings of political causation change, under what conditions they change, and whether those changes subsequently shape policy choices.

Connection to my research

This argument connects strategic-narrative research to foreign-policy decision-making. It shifts the dependent variable from visible alignment to the conceptual structures through which elites diagnose problems and justify responses.

The approach could be developed through three analytical stages:

  1. Reconstruct the causal structure of Chinese official narratives concerning development, security, sovereignty, modernization, and international order.

  2. Compare Iranian and Saudi elite causal maps across time to identify changes, continuities, and alternative causal structures.

  3. Process-trace selected policy decisions to determine whether observed changes resulted from Chinese narrative exposure, material incentives, strategic hedging, or pre-existing domestic beliefs.

This framework builds on From Development Partner to Order-Shaping Actor: China’s Strategic Narrative in the Middle East but moves from narrative projection to cognitive reception. It also offers a stricter definition of ideational influence: an observable change in the causal beliefs through which decision-makers understand political relationships.

Limitations

LIMITATION


Similar causal language does not by itself prove Chinese influence. Chinese and foreign elites may independently adopt comparable arguments because they face similar problems, draw on shared developmental traditions, or respond to common structural conditions.

Cognitive maps reconstructed from public speeches may also reflect strategic communication rather than private belief. Elite discourse must therefore be compared across internal documents, interviews, policy debates, institutional records, and decision-making episodes wherever evidence is available.

It may also be difficult to establish when a causal belief entered elite reasoning. Chinese narratives can reinforce existing beliefs rather than create new ones. In such cases, influence may consist of validation, consolidation, or reframing rather than conversion.

Finally, changes in causal beliefs do not automatically determine policy outcomes. Material constraints, bureaucratic competition, alliance commitments, leadership interests, and domestic politics may prevent ideational changes from being translated into policy. The argument therefore treats causal-belief change as a possible mechanism of influence, not as a sufficient explanation of foreign-policy behaviour.

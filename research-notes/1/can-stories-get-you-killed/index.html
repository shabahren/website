Research Note · Research Note 1

Can Stories Get You Killed? Narrative Unfalsifiability and Strategic Entrapment

Considers whether political narratives become strategically dangerous by preventing actors from recognizing failure; proposes 'narrative unfalsifiability' as a link to weak strategic learning and conflict persistence.

Published 28 July 2026
  • Strategic Narratives
Research connections

ConceptsSelf-Sealing Strategic Narratives

Research problem

Can political stories become strategically dangerous?

States and political movements need narratives to explain who they are, what threatens them, why sacrifices are necessary, and what political future they seek. Narratives can support legitimacy, collective action, and continuity under pressure.1 Yet a coherent narrative may not necessarily be strategically useful.

This note explores the possibility that some narratives become unfalsifiable: no observable outcome is allowed to count as evidence against their central claims. Military defeat can be described as moral victory, diplomatic isolation as proof of righteousness, and failed predictions as evidence that victory merely requires more time.

The research problem is therefore not whether narratives are simply true or false. It is whether they preserve the conditions under which actors can recognize error, revise causal beliefs, and change strategy.

METHOD

This is a concept-development note rather than a completed study. The argument would need to be examined through comparative analysis of how political actors respond to events that contradict their own stated expectations.

Developing argument

An adaptable narrative separates foundational identity commitments from particular strategies, instruments, and timelines. An actor may preserve a sovereignty claim, national identity, or historical purpose while revising the means through which it pursues them.

A self-sealing narrative instead protects its central assumptions by reinterpreting contradictory evidence. Both success and failure may therefore be treated as confirmation:

Success→narrative confirmation \text{Success} \rightarrow \text{narrative confirmation} Success→narrative confirmation Failure→narrative confirmation \text{Failure} \rightarrow \text{narrative confirmation} Failure→narrative confirmation

Possible indicators include moving definitions of victory, indefinite temporal deferral, converting material loss into symbolic victory, delegitimizing critics, and treating compromise as betrayal.

The main speculation is that narrative coherence is strategically beneficial only when combined with revisability. Narratives may help political actors endure uncertainty, but they may also prevent learning from adverse outcomes.

Ontological-security theory may help explain this process. Political actors seek continuity in their understandings of themselves and their environments.2 Familiar conflicts and routines may consequently be maintained even when they impose physical or material costs.3 Revising a strategy may become particularly difficult when that strategy has been incorporated into collective identity.

Ontological security does not necessarily produce rigidity, however. Identity continuity could enable policy adjustment when leaders can represent change as consistent with foundational values. The relevant distinction may be between preserving identity through adaptation and preserving identity by denying strategic failure.

Domestic politics may influence which pathway develops. Protracted conflict can strengthen military institutions, security bureaucracies, political parties, ideological organizations, and other actors whose authority or organizational identity is connected to the conflict.4 These actors may gain privileged authority to define threats, victory, sacrifice, and betrayal.

A possible causal pathway is:

Protracted conflict strengthens conflict-specialist actors; these actors acquire narrative authority; acknowledging failure threatens both their preferred strategy and institutional position; adverse evidence is therefore reinterpreted rather than used to revise policy.

This need not involve deliberate deception. Political actors may sincerely understand their institution, strategy, and national survival as inseparable.

Preliminary literature review

Ontological-security scholarship explains why political actors seek stable identities, routines, and biographical continuity.2 It also suggests that familiar but harmful relationships may sometimes be preferred to disruptive uncertainty.3 Later research has developed the roles of anxiety, relational identity, organizations, and state–society relations in producing ontological security.4

Research connecting narrative and ontological security examines how foreign-policy narratives preserve identity continuity and how narrative change may enable or constrain policy transformation.5 Strategic-narrative scholarship focuses more broadly on how actors construct system, identity, and issue narratives to shape political understanding and legitimate action.1

Work on intractable conflict demonstrates how collective memory, conflict-supporting beliefs, and institutionalized cultures of conflict can normalize continued confrontation.6 Studies of war termination and rhetorical “victory traps” further suggest that political narratives can make achievable victory difficult to define and unsuccessful outcomes difficult to acknowledge.7

These literatures provide components of the proposed argument, but they do not fully establish when strategic narratives retain identifiable failure conditions and when they become progressively insulated from counterevidence. This remains a question for further research.

Connection to my research

This idea connects ontological security and strategic narratives to the study of strategic learning in protracted sovereignty conflicts. It could support comparative research on China–Taiwan, Northern Ireland, and Israel–Palestine without assuming in advance that any case contains a fully self-sealing narrative.

Limitations

LIMITATION

Conflict persistence alone cannot demonstrate narrative unfalsifiability. Material power, deterrence, external support, geography, institutional incentives, and bargaining problems may independently explain continued conflict.

The concept would need to be identified through actors’ responses to specific disconfirming events. Research must also distinguish ordinary narrative adaptation from the systematic elimination of all conceivable failure conditions.
